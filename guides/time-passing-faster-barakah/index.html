When the Days Feel Like Hours: A Reflection on the Passing of Time and the Blessing of Life

Published August 10, 2026 · 12 min read

Have you ever felt that the past few years have gone by faster than they should?

A week begins, and almost before you notice, it is over. Ramadan arrives and we welcome it, only to find ourselves suddenly approaching its final nights. Children grow older, faces change, seasons pass, and sometimes we look back in disbelief and wonder: When did all that time go?

What makes this feeling even more striking is that we live in an age filled with technologies designed to save time. Cars shorten journeys. Phones erase distances. The internet allows us to complete in minutes what once required days. And yet, many of us feel that we have less time than ever.

It is almost as though every minute we save is immediately claimed by ten new things demanding our attention.

For a Muslim, this feeling brings to mind a remarkable saying of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ about a time when time itself would seem to draw closer and pass more quickly.

“Time Will Pass Rapidly”

The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

“The Hour will not be established until knowledge is taken away, earthquakes become frequent, and time passes rapidly…”

— Sahih al-Bukhari

Another narration describes it in striking terms:

“The Hour will not be established until time draws close: a year will be like a month, a month like a week, a week like a day, a day like an hour, and an hour like the burning of a flame.”

— Jami‘ al-Tirmidhi

It is a powerful image. A year feels like a month. A month like a week. A day like an hour.

Classical scholars discussed several possible meanings of this “drawing close” of time. One of the most commonly mentioned explanations is the diminishing of barakah — blessing — in time, so that a person may find that much less is accomplished within the same number of hours.

Some scholars also understood the hadith as pointing to a more literal form of the shortening or rapid passing of time near the end of the world.

We should therefore be careful not to claim that every personal feeling that “time is flying” is necessarily the direct fulfillment of this prophecy. But the hadith invites us to consider something deeply important: A day may still contain twenty-four hours, while the blessing within those hours becomes very small.

Twenty-Four Hours — Yet Not All Days Are Equal

Every person receives the same twenty-four hours in a day. But are all twenty-four-hour days really equal?

One person may live a single day and leave it having learned something useful, prayed with sincerity, completed meaningful work, brought happiness to their parents, quietly given charity, or spoken a kind word that remained in another person’s heart.

The same day may pass for someone else through endless scrolling, videos they barely remember, news that changes nothing in their life, arguments that benefit no one, and constant postponement of the things they know truly matter.

Both people lived through the same number of hours. But one lived the day. The other merely passed through it.

This brings us closer to understanding the meaning of barakah. Blessing in time does not necessarily mean receiving more hours. It means that Allah places greater good and benefit within the hours you already have — so that your time produces something meaningful in your faith, your work, your family, your character, and your life.

The Prophet ﷺ prayed:

“O Allah, bless my nation in their early mornings.”

— Abu Dawud and al-Tirmidhi

That is barakah. And it cannot be measured by the hands of a clock alone.

In Reality, We Are Made of Days

We usually measure our lives in years. We say: I am thirty, forty, or sixty years old. But a lifetime is not simply a number written on an identity card. Your life is the hour you are living right now.

Every day that ends does not simply disappear from the calendar. It takes a piece of our lifetime with it — a piece that will never return.

Allah swears by time in one of the shortest and most profound chapters of the Qur’an:

“By Time. Indeed, mankind is in loss — except those who believe, do righteous deeds, encourage one another to the truth, and encourage one another to patience.”

— Qur’an, Surah Al-‘Asr 103:1–3

In just a few verses, the entire reality of human life is placed before us. Time is passing. Our capital is being spent. And we are not saved from that loss simply by living for many years. We are saved by what we place inside those years: faith, righteous deeds, truth, and patience.

The problem is not that time passes. Time was created to pass. The tragedy is when it passes and leaves nothing worthwhile behind.

“Two Blessings Many People Waste”

The Prophet ﷺ said:

“There are two blessings which many people waste: health and free time.”

— Sahih al-Bukhari

We often understand the true value of something only after we lose it. A person may once have had a healthy body with which they could stand in prayer, walk, travel, work, serve their parents, and help others — only to understand the value of health after becoming ill.

Another person may have had evenings, weekends, and years in which they repeatedly said, “I will do it later.” Then a day comes when they would give almost anything for one hour of that old “free time.”

Free time, therefore, is not always an empty space that needs to be killed. Sometimes it is a blessing waiting to be invested.

We often say: “I’m just killing time.” But time is not our enemy. Time is our life.

How Does Blessing Disappear From a Busy Life?

Being busy is not the same as being productive. A person can fill every minute of the day and still go to bed wondering what they actually accomplished.

One notification leads to a message. The message leads to a video. The video leads to another. Then comes a news story, a comment, an argument, another notification — and eventually we return to the task we originally intended to do after our attention has been broken ten times.

We may live in an age of abundant information while suffering from a severe shortage of attention. We see countless things but contemplate very little. We read constantly but learn surprisingly little. We communicate with dozens of people while sometimes neglecting those sitting closest to us.

We may know what happened thousands of miles away today while having no idea what is happening inside the heart of a parent sitting in the next room.

Technology itself is not the problem. It can be an extraordinary blessing when used well. The problem begins when the tools that were supposed to save our time quietly become the things consuming our lives.

You Will Be Asked About Your Lifetime

The Messenger of Allah ﷺ said:

“The feet of a servant will not move on the Day of Resurrection until he is asked about his lifetime and how he spent it…”

— Jami‘ al-Tirmidhi

Notice the question. Not simply: How long did you live? But: How did you spend your life?

A lifetime is something that is gradually consumed. Every minute can be spent only once. Money can be lost and earned again. Possessions can disappear and sometimes be replaced. But an hour that has left your life can never be purchased back.

This transforms our understanding of time. It is no longer merely a number on a screen. It is a trust.

Islam Does Not Ask You to Abandon the World

Using your time well does not mean spending every waking hour inside a mosque or withdrawing from ordinary life. Useful work is part of a meaningful life. Learning is part of it. Raising children is part of it. Caring for your parents is part of it. Earning an honest living is part of it. Becoming better at your profession is part of it. Helping another person is part of it. Resting so that your body and mind can continue their journey is part of it.

Even a kind word, a smile, or time spent with your family can become an act of worship when accompanied by a sincere intention.

Allah says:

“Seek, through what Allah has given you, the Home of the Hereafter, but do not forget your share of this world. And do good as Allah has done good to you.”

— Qur’an, Al-Qasas 28:77

Islam does not call us to escape from this world. It teaches us how to live within it properly — building what benefits ourselves and others, while never forgetting where the journey ultimately leads.

Do Not Wait Until “I Have More Time”

One of the easiest promises to make to ourselves is: “When I have more time, I will read.” “When life becomes quieter, I will spend more time with the Qur’an.” “Later, I will visit my parents more often.” “I will start taking care of my health.” “One day, I will learn that skill.” “I will repent when my life becomes more settled.”

But life rarely comes to us and says: Your responsibilities are finished now. You may begin. One difficulty ends and another appears. One responsibility finishes and another begins.

The Prophet ﷺ advised us to take advantage of what we possess before circumstances change:

“Take advantage of five before five: your youth before your old age, your health before your illness, your wealth before your poverty, your free time before your busyness, and your life before your death.”

— al-Hakim and al-Bayhaqi; graded authentic by a number of scholars

The essential word is: Take advantage. Do not wait for perfect circumstances. Begin with what you have now.

Ten Minutes May Be More Valuable Than You Think

You do not need to transform your entire life tomorrow. Ten minutes of Qur’an every day may, over the years, build a relationship with Allah that did not exist before. Thirty minutes each day spent learning a useful skill may change your professional future. A short phone call to your mother or father may become one of the most valuable moments of your day. Two quiet rak‘ahs may repair something in your heart that hours of noise have damaged. A daily walk may protect your health. A small but consistent charity may be waiting for you on a day when you desperately need a single good deed.

The Prophet ﷺ said:

“The deeds most beloved to Allah are those that are consistent, even if they are small.”

— Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim

The value of an action is not always measured by its size. Sometimes the greatest blessing is hidden inside something small that continues.

Ask Yourself a Different Question

At the end of the day, instead of asking only: What did I do today? Try asking: What did I do today that I would be happy to find waiting for me when I meet Allah?

Then ask another question: What did I do today that could make my life — or someone else’s life — better tomorrow?

In those two questions, faith and worldly life meet. A prayer you performed. Something useful you learned. Work you completed with excellence. A mistake you corrected. A body you cared for. Parents you honored. A child you taught. Someone in need you helped. A heart you comforted.

These things may appear small inside a crowded day. But they are the things from which a meaningful life is built.

“My Lord, Send Me Back”

Perhaps one of the most sobering truths about time is that its full value may become clearest only when a person’s share of it is coming to an end. Allah describes that moment:

“When death approaches any of them, they cry, ‘My Lord! Let me go back, so I may do good in what I left behind.’”

— Qur’an, Al-Mu’minun 23:99–100

The person does not ask for more possessions. They do not ask for a larger house. They do not ask for another opportunity to watch something they missed. They ask for time. More time to do good.

But the moment in which the true value of time becomes perfectly clear is also the moment when returning is no longer possible. And that is why there is something extraordinary about the fact that you are reading these words now: You still have time.

Do Not Fear the Speed of the Days — Notice It

Reflecting on the rapid passing of time and the signs of the Last Hour is not meant to make a believer live in constant fear, watching the calendar with anxiety. It is meant to awaken the heart. To remind us that this world was never meant to be our permanent home. That even a long life is short when we look back upon it. And that Allah, in His mercy, has not asked us to accomplish impossible things. He asks us to know where we are going — and to keep moving toward Him through sincere, consistent good.

Allah says:

“O you who believe! Be mindful of Allah, and let every soul look to what it has sent forth for tomorrow.”

— Qur’an, Al-Hashr 59:18

There is something powerful about the word “tomorrow.” We spend much of our lives preparing for tomorrow in this world. The Qur’an reminds us to prepare for another tomorrow as well.

Before You Leave This Page

Do not allow this article to become just one more thing you read before moving on to the next thing. Stop for a moment. Think about the past week. How many of its hours can you actually remember? And how many disappeared without you even knowing where they went?

Then do not spend too long regretting what has already passed. Look instead at what remains. Pray the next prayer with a present heart. Read a page of the Qur’an. Finish something worthwhile that you have been postponing. Learn something useful. Call your parents. Sit with your children without a phone in your hand. Forgive someone. Correct a mistake. Plant something that may continue benefiting others after you are gone.

Perhaps we cannot make the days move more slowly. But, by the permission of Allah, we can make sure they pass carrying something worth keeping.

The clock stops for no one. Life does not move backwards. And every new sunrise is more than the beginning of another day. It is another piece of your lifetime that Allah has allowed you to witness.

So perhaps the most important question is not: How much time do I have left? But rather: How much good can I place into the time that remains?
